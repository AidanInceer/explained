A model learns only what is in its data. Before choosing a model, the most important questions are where the data comes from, what it represents, and what it leaves out. Most of the effort in real machine learning projects goes into data, not models.

## Where data comes from

- **Collected in your own product**: clicks, purchases, support tickets, sensor readings. Closest to the real task, and often messy, biased towards current users, and subject to privacy law.
- **Public datasets**: benchmark collections such as ImageNet for images, or web crawls such as Common Crawl for text. Large and free, with known problems and licences to check.
- **Scraped**: collected from websites by your own crawler. Legal and ethical questions about copyright, terms of use and personal data come with it.
- **Bought or licensed**: from data vendors or publishers. Check exactly what you may use it for.
- **Made**: written by people for the purpose, or generated by other models, as the lesson [Augmentation and synthetic data](/lesson/augmentation) explains.

## What large models are trained on

Large language models are pretrained on trillions of tokens: filtered web pages, books, code, scientific papers, and more. Collecting, cleaning and filtering this data, removing spam, duplicates and harmful content, choosing the mix, is a major part of building such a model, as the lesson on pretraining explains.

## Questions to ask of any dataset

Researchers proposed that every dataset should come with a **datasheet**, answering:

- **Why** was it created, and by whom?
- **What** is in it? How many examples, what kinds, what is missing?
- **How** was it collected? From where, over what time, by what method?
- **How** was it labelled? By whom, with what instructions, how consistently?
- **Who** is represented, and who is not?
- **What** may it be used for? What are its licence and privacy limits?

These answers tell you whether a model trained on the data will work for your users.

## The data must match the use

A model works on inputs like those it was trained on. Watch for gaps:

- **Different population**: a model trained on one hospital's patients, used in another country.
- **Different time**: data from before a change in prices, laws or habits.
- **Different conditions**: studio photos for training, phone photos in use.

When live inputs drift away from the training data, quality drops, often without any error message. Monitor it.

> **Rule of thumb.** Look at the data. Read a few hundred examples by hand before you train anything. You will find duplicates, wrong labels, odd formats and missing groups that no summary statistic shows.

## Remember

- Data comes from your product, public sets, scraping, purchase, or is made.
- Check licence, privacy and consent for every source.
- Ask why, what, how, by whom and for whom a dataset was made.
- The data must match the use, and you should read examples by hand.
